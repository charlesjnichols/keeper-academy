## (Foundational / Introduction)

# Age Considerations & Developmental Reality

When we're working with kids between 6 and 9, let's be honest we aren't trying to win the World Cup on Saturday mornings.  We’re trying to make sure these kids fall in love with moving their bodies and kicking a ball.

At ages 6 to 7, children naturally view sports egocentrically; their focus is almost entirely on the ball, creating the classic "beehive" cluster. By ages 8 to 9, their spatial awareness expands to recognize basic relationships between teammates, opponents, and open space.  Full-field play (like 5v5 or 7v7) overwhelms young players because ball touches are too few and the field is too big.

---

# Coaching Approach & Small-Sided Games

Our biggest job at this stage is **emotional safety**. When a kid lets a soft goal in and looks over at the sideline with wide eyes, we need to be the ones smiling and telling them it's all good. We've also got to bring parents along, reminding them that weekend scorelines matter far less than whether kids are smiling on the car ride home.

To bridge the developmental gap, we rely heavily on small-sided games and adjacent activities. By stripping away complex rules and exaggerating specific tactical scenarios, kids discover spatial solutions naturally through play rather than rigid lectures. **They learn through play** because play is their native language. When children experiment, fail, laugh, and figure out space on their own terms without an adult screaming instructions from the sideline, the lesson actually sticks. The ball becomes their best teacher.

---

# The Case Against Early Specialization

And here’s a hill worth dying on: at 6 to 9 years old, **no one is a full-time goalie.** It breaks our heart when we see a seven-year-old stuck between the posts for every minute of every game while their friends run up the field. That’s a fast track to burnout.

Youth sports dropout rates are brutal; kids walk away when they lose the fun, face too much adult pressure, or get boxed into a single role too early. Instead, let's rotate everyone through the position during practice so every player experiences what it feels like back there, building deep, genuine empathy across the team. For games, stepping in goal should always be voluntary, never forced. Above all, remember: at this age, we aren't specialists—we're building athletes!! Keep it fun, keep it shared, and let them play.
